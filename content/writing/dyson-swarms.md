## Opening: why ask

- The tech tree's far end is dated by energy, and its last step is a [Dyson swarm](/apps/tech-tree/#n=dyson-swarm). Dates are one question; what it would physically be is another, and more fun.
- Numbers for scale: the Sun puts out 3.8 × 10²⁶ W. Earth intercepts about 1.7 × 10¹⁷ W of that, about one part in two billion. Humanity used about 2 × 10¹³ W in 2025, roughly a ten-thousandth of the sunlight hitting Earth.
- Freeman Dyson's 1960 paper ("Search for artificial stellar sources of infrared radiation," *Science*) was really about how to *detect* such a thing: anything using a star's light has to shed it as waste heat, so it would glow in the infrared. He later said he meant a loose collection of objects on independent orbits, not a solid shell; the shell version came from science fiction.

- **Shell.** A rigid sphere fails twice. The compressive stress on a shell at Earth's distance far exceeds any known material's strength. And by Newton's shell theorem the Sun pulls on a uniform shell with no net force, so nothing keeps it centered: any nudge grows until it hits the Sun. A ringworld has the same instability in the plane.
- **Swarm.** Independent collectors, each in an ordinary orbit. No exotic materials, it can be built one piece at a time, and every piece is useful the day it's launched. This is what almost every serious proposal means.
- **Bubble (statites).** A sail light enough is pushed outward by sunlight as hard as gravity pulls it in, at any distance, since both fall off with the square of distance. The break-even is about 0.8 g/m² for a sail that absorbs and 1.5 g/m² for one that reflects (L ÷ 4πcGM; Robert Forward patented the idea in 1993). They can sit still in any arrangement, but that's thinner than household plastic wrap, with no room for much machinery.
- Your point: what gets built likely depends on materials and coordination, and in practice it would start as an incomplete swarm and might end as a mix: dense rings of orbiters, with statites filling the gaps above and below.

- Your incremental-stages point, made concrete. Each stage is useful on its own, which is why the build would be gradual rather than a single project:
  1. [Power beamed down from orbit](/apps/tech-tree/#n=space-based-solar) (megawatts; China plans demonstrations in the 2030s).
  2. [A factory in space that copies itself](/apps/tech-tree/#n=self-replicating-space-factory) from local material (NASA's 1980 lunar factory study: a 100-tonne seed doubling in about a year).
  3. [More power in space than Earth used in 2025](/apps/tech-tree/#n=space-power-terawatts) (20 TW, around 50,000 km² of collectors near Earth, about the area of Costa Rica).
  4. [Kardashev Type I](/apps/tech-tree/#n=kardashev-type-1) (10¹⁶ W in all; mostly in space, since Earth's surface can't shed that much heat).
  5. [More power in space than all the sunlight on Earth](/apps/tech-tree/#n=sunlight-on-earth-in-space).
  6. [One percent of the Sun](/apps/tech-tree/#n=sun-one-percent).
  7. [Most of the Sun](/apps/tech-tree/#n=dyson-swarm).
- Material: Mercury is the usual quarry (close to the Sun, metal-rich, weak gravity, no air). Half its mass spread over a sphere at its orbit is a layer about half a millimeter of iron thick. Armstrong and Sandberg (2013) work out a self-copying build using up Mercury in about 31 years, most of it in the last few, since each doubling takes the same time but adds more. Bolton (2026) argues heat at Mercury's surface could slow mining a lot. The asteroids have far less mass (the main belt is about 3% of the Moon) but enough for very thin collectors.
- Doublings: from a 100 MW factory to half the Sun is about 61 doublings. At one doubling a year that's 61 years; at one every few months, about 15. This is why the timeline is about the *doubling time*, not the size.

- What the tree says now: see the chart; the swarm's best guess is about 2180, with 80 percent between about 2100 and 2470.
- Your question about superintelligence. The model used to let growth stay slow forever in 45% of runs, which put a quarter of runs past 3000. Now the "held back" course lasts decades (median 60 years, tail of centuries) and then the explosion comes anyway: over a century or more, rules change, rivals defect, and machines that build machines find ways around what held them. That pulled the median from about 2240 to about 2155 in the model, and the 90th percentile from after 5000 to about 2330.
- What superintelligence can and can't shorten: design, coordination and the engineering of self-copying machines, yes; but each doubling still has to mine, refine and lift material and radiate heat away. Physical doubling times (bacteria: minutes; fast-growing plants: days; factories today: years) set the floor, not cleverness.
- What could make it later or never: a decision not to build one (it dims the Sun for Earth if badly arranged, and it consumes a planet), a setback, or simply no demand. Murphy's "Do the Math" (2011) point: at 2.3% growth, we'd need the whole Sun in about 1,350 years, so growth has to stop *somewhere*; the question is whether it stops before or after the swarm.

- The geometry problem: two circular orbits at the *same* distance but different tilts cross at two points, so collectors there would eventually collide. Orbits at *different* distances never cross. So a swarm is a thick shell of many rings, each at its own distance (Starlink does the same in Earth orbit, with shells at different altitudes).
- Within a ring, everything moves at the same speed, so spacing holds by itself, like cars in one lane at one speed.
- Perturbations (planets, light pressure, each other's gravity, impacts) are corrected by tilting the collector against sunlight, a solar sail's steering, so station-keeping needs no propellant.
- Shading: outer rings would shade inner ones and vice versa. Collectors can be arranged so most of them see the Sun most of the time, and the whole thing has to leave Earth's line to the Sun clear (or Earth gets eclipsed). Worth saying: a complete swarm is a choice about what Earth's sky looks like.
- Risk: a collision cascade like the Kessler syndrome in Earth orbit. Many tiny independent units fail gracefully; one cascade could also take out a ring.

## Maintenance

- Hazards: micrometeoroids, solar wind and radiation degrading cells, heat cycling, and wear on moving parts.
- Likely answer: don't repair, replace. Units are cheap and identical; robots collect dead ones and melt them down for new ones. The same self-copying industry that builds the swarm maintains it, so its "maintenance budget" is a few percent of its production.
- Intelligence: each unit needs little; coordination (traffic control for trillions of objects) is the real software problem, and a good job for the kind of AI that would exist by then.

- **Used in place** is most likely: computing (Bradbury's "Matrioshka brain," 1997: nested shells of computers, each running on the waste heat of the one inside), refining and manufacturing, building more collectors.
- **Beamed** with lasers or microwaves. Beams spread with distance, so sending power across the solar system needs very large mirrors or antennas; good for pushing light sails (Breakthrough Starshot wanted a 100 GW laser for gram-scale probes) and for launching probes to other stars (Armstrong and Sandberg's "eternity in six hours": the whole build is about launching probes to other galaxies).
- **Stored**: there's little need, since the Sun doesn't set for a swarm. Energy would be stored mainly to ship it (fuel, metals, maybe antimatter, which is very inefficient to make).
- **What it's all for**, and whether it's needed: the case for is minds and computation (more people, digital or otherwise, and more thinking per person), science, and expansion beyond the solar system. The case against: needs may saturate, efficiency improves, and nobody is obliged to use the Sun. Your take here.
- **Waste heat and detectability**: radiators at room temperature glow at about 10 μm. Searches: Project Hephaistos (Suazo et al., 2024) found seven candidate stars with excess infrared out of about 5 million; follow-up suggested background galaxies behind at least some of them. Tabby's star (2015) dimmed oddly, but it turned out to be dust.
