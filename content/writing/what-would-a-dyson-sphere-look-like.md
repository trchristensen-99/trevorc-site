I've recently been working on a [Real Life Tech Tree](/projects/real-life-tech-tree), and one of the furthest milestones from the present day is a [Dyson sphere](/apps/tech-tree/#n=dyson-swarm). In case you aren't familiar, a Dyson sphere is essentially a shell of material around a star that allows you to capture almost all of its useful energy. The term "Dyson swarm" is more descriptive of how it would actually look, which you can see in the diagram below — Dyson was referring to such a swarm with his original sphere concept, even though the swarm would almost certainly not be an actual sphere.

<figcaption>Three ways to surround a star. A rigid shell fails: no material is strong enough to hold it up, and nothing keeps it centered on the Sun, so any nudge grows until it hits. A swarm is many separate collectors, each in its own orbit, and is what nearly every serious proposal means. A bubble is a cloud of sails light enough (under about 1.5 grams per square meter, thinner than plastic wrap) that sunlight pushes them out as hard as gravity pulls them in, so they can hover without orbiting.</figcaption>

There are a few key prerequisites to being able to construct enough power collectors to almost entirely capture the energy from a star. The most important one would be factories in space that are able to copy themselves and autonomously manufacture any relevant products, as this would enable you to scale up quickly. Since the swarm can be built incrementally, these self-replicating factories could be among the swarm's first members, using energy from the sun and materials from elsewhere in the solar system to power the construction of more factories in a virtuous cycle. It is also important to be able to use, store, and transport the power however necessary. As always, AI can give this a massive boost as it gets better.

Here are some visualizations of what the rings of satellites would look like: the key feature is that each orbit is slightly more tilted and slightly larger than the next orbit further in. You could actually use the energy from the sun as a way to steer the power satellites as they move, which is a neat trick. Seen from the Sun, though, the rings overlap where they cross each other, and there the outer collectors are shaded by the inner ones, so it helps to spread those crossing points around rather than lining them all up as in the diagrams. A single orbit could also have two or more layers of satellites, so that there is less risk of collision.

One of my worries was that if we built a Dyson swarm in our own solar system we would block out the sun from Earth and the other planets, but you could just program the collectors to turn 90 degrees to let almost all of the sunlight through wherever it was needed. The diagram below shows much wider holes than you would actually have to make. Excitingly, you could actually use the collectors as mirrors to precisely tune the amount of sunlight a planet receives. For instance, you could block most of the sunlight from Venus to cool it down or redirect more to Mars to heat it up (or do this to other planets in another solar system if you want to keep the ones in ours as-is). The combination of solar energy tuning and the incredible amounts of energy captured by the swarm would unlock amazing new abilities to terraform planets.[^1]

A major risk with such a Dyson swarm is that a failure in one single panel could cause a crash and a subsequent chain reaction from debris that would take out vast swathes of the swarm, but there are ways to mitigate this.

You might wonder why we need all of this power, besides relatively far-out applications like terraforming. Fundamentally, more energy allows us to do more useful work of every sort, whether that involves giving people more resources, curing disease, discovering new things about the universe, or any number of other applications you could think of. This assumes that we have ways to harness this energy, but even if these methods are super inefficient, unlocking so much energy would make those inefficiencies not that big of a deal, and we could prioritize improving efficiency once any needs that are more pressing have been met. In my humble opinion, Dyson swarms represent one of the coolest future technologies that we can concretely think about right now, and I hope I live to see one built.
